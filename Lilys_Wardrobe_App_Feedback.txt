Reference reviewed: - Title: MY PETITE STYLE — Product Requirements
Document - Subtitle: Petite-first AI personal stylist for iPhone and

Coverage and limitation: The PRD was reviewed from beginning through end

2. MY TOP THREE PRIORITIES

Priority 1 — Let me tell the app what I need TODAY

Lily’s words: “It has to let me tell it what I want/need for example
bloated or not or rainy or not etc.”

Summary: Before styling, Lily wants to provide situational context that
changes what is appropriate today. This includes: - Occasion. -
Weather/conditions such as rainy or sunny. - Whether she feels bloated
or not, so the app can adjust fitted vs. looser suggestions. - A
preferred or required color, such as blue for a themed event. - A
starting garment she wants to wear. - Other immediate constraints.

Importance: Essential.

Priority 2 — Styling must be intentional and varied

Lily’s words: “It has to be intentional with the styling. Not always
give me the same 2-3 colors in different formats.”

Summary: Lily already knows how to make safe outfits with denim, black,
and same-family colors. The app adds value when it introduces
combinations she would not immediately choose herself, while keeping
them coordinated, wearable, chic, elegant, and intentional.

Examples Lily gave: - Blue + light pink: interesting and desirable. -
Olive green pants + brick-colored shirt: unexpected but coordinated. -
Strong red + green: likely too oppositional.

Importance: Essential.

Priority 3 — Let me visually edit an outfit piece by piece

Lily’s words: “It has to let me be able to change an item.”

Summary: Lily wants visual outfit options in which she can tap an
individual garment and change it—especially its color—without losing the
rest of the outfit. She does not want to regenerate everything simply
because one piece or color is wrong.

Importance: Essential.

5. DISAGREEMENTS WITH PLAN

Disagreement 1 — Replace Creative/Risky as a permanent third lane

PRD section/requirement ID: Section 6, FR-06; FR-07.

Current plan: Every successful generation returns exactly three
directions: Safe/Simple, Stylish/Elevated, and Creative/Risky.

What I need: Three options with different purposes: 1. Safe/Simple using
what I own. 2. Stylish/Elevated using what I own. 3. Stylish/Elevated
incorporating a missing purchasable piece when shopping is appropriate.

Concrete example: For blue dress pants: 1. Blue pants + matching blue
cardigan (safe). 2. Blue pants + light pink blouse from current closet
(elevated/current). 3. Blue pants + a complementary missing item,
clearly marked as not owned, with an option to find/buy/save it.

Importance: Essential.

Status: Conflict.

Clarification or proposed change: Replace the fixed Creative/Risky lane
for Lily’s experience. Unexpected color combinations can still occur in
the elevated lane; “not risky” does not mean “only neutral.” Shopping
should remain optional and should not fabricate ownership. If the user
has requested strict wardrobe-only mode or does not have time/want to
shop, the third lane should not silently violate that constraint.
Developer/product decision is still required on how to reconcile this
with the PRD’s strict inventory rules.

Disagreement 2 — Primary outfit visualization should be Lily wearing it

PRD section/requirement ID: FR-08; FR-20; Generate Look workflow in the
try-on/image section.

Current plan: Core results are visual outfit cards/boards. Generate Look
is optional and separate; a body photo is not required for ordinary
styling.

What I need: When Lily has chosen to provide a reusable body/reference
photo and enabled this experience, she wants the initial three outfit
choices shown on her body, not primarily as flat garment boards.

Concrete example: Instead of seeing separate pictures of blue pants, a
pink blouse, and shoes, Lily wants to compare three images of herself
wearing the three proposed looks.

Importance: Essential for Lily’s preferred experience; body-photo
participation itself remains optional/user-controlled.

Status: Conflict / scope difference.

Clarification or proposed change: Explore an opt-in “visual-first/on-me”
mode for Lily. Do not remove the privacy-preserving non-photo core for
users who do not want body-image processing. Technical cost, latency,
supported garment types, accuracy, provider terms, and consent gates
remain feasibility/security decisions. The app must not claim the
preview proves fit.

Disagreement 3 — Exact 25/26-inch inseam is not confirmed

PRD section/requirement ID: FR-01; Section 3.1; Section 26 Lily fit
details.

Current plan: The PRD records Lily’s “initial inseam preference” as 25
or 26 inches.

What I need: Treat inseam as unknown unless Lily later measures it or
identifies a known well-fitting garment with a confirmed inseam.

Concrete example: Lily said she has never measured her inseam and does
not know what an inseam is. What she can confirm is that pants are
commonly too long.

Importance: Essential data-quality correction.

Status: Conflict.

Clarification or proposed change: Remove 25/26 from Lily-confirmed data
or explicitly mark it unverified provenance requiring confirmation. Do
not use it as a sizing rule.

Disagreement 4 — Automatic wear-history repetition control is not a user priority

PRD section/requirement ID: FR-39.

Current plan: Use recent local outfit history to reduce repeats.

What I need: Lily does not need detailed tracking of exactly when she
wore an outfit. She prefers organizing saved outfits by where/why she
wears them and intentionally choosing past looks.

Concrete example: A Work collection containing outfits with her blue
dress pants is more useful to her than “you wore this Tuesday, so I
won’t show it again.”

Importance: Preference.

Status: Conflict / simplification opportunity.

Clarification or proposed change: Keep repeat-reduction subtle or
optional for Lily. Prioritize user-created collections and context
retrieval over detailed wear chronology in her UI.

6. MISSING NEEDS OR FEATURES

Missing/unclear 1 — Daily comfort/body-feel input

PRD section/requirement ID: FR-05 supports “special constraints”; FR-01
stores fit preferences.

Current plan: Free-text constraints can technically carry this
information, but a clear daily comfort input was not identified in the
reviewed styling flow.

What I need: A quick way to say whether Lily feels bloated/not bloated
or otherwise wants a less-fitted/more-fitted outfit today.

Concrete example: “I’m bloated today” should steer the current request
toward silhouettes she feels comfortable wearing without permanently
changing her body/fit profile.

Importance: Essential.

Status: Unclear / potentially missing as a first-class control.

Clarification or proposed change: Treat this as per-request comfort
context, not a permanent body judgment or inferred health state. A
simple optional chip/control or free-text constraint could satisfy the
need.

Missing/unclear 2 — User-created outfit collections/tabs

PRD section/requirement ID: FR-13 supports saved outfits and optional
date/occasion tags; FR-39 supports search.

Current plan: Saved looks can be tagged, but Lily specifically wants
persistent user-created tabs/collections.

What I need: Create and name collections such as Work, Gym, Going Out,
etc., then choose where a saved outfit belongs and browse those
collections later.

Concrete example: When Lily does not want to experiment before work, she
opens Work and chooses from previously successful looks.

Importance: Preference (high-value).

Status: Unclear / missing as explicit UX.

Clarification or proposed change: Consider user-created collections or a
collection-like saved-outfit filter. Avoid forcing detailed wear
tracking to achieve this.

Missing/unclear 3 — Visual versatility proof before buying

PRD section/requirement ID: FR-12; Section 13.

Current plan: Find matching real products with
fit/size/length/price/stock/delivery evidence.

What I need: Show how many useful combinations a proposed purchase can
make, especially with Lily’s existing closet, and show visual examples
before she buys.

Concrete example: “This beige cardigan works with these six looks you
can make from your closet,” accompanied by outfit previews.

Importance: Preference (important for purchase decisions).

Status: Missing / unclear.

Clarification or proposed change: Add a “How I’d wear it” / “Works with
my closet” action. Any numeric versatility score should be explainable
and based on known wardrobe inventory rather than pretending the app
knows items that have not been entered.

Missing/unclear 4 — Affordable vs. higher-end shopping comparison

PRD section/requirement ID: FR-12; Section 13.

Current plan: Product search respects fit preferences,
price/stock/delivery, and budget.

What I need: When shopping, Lily would like two meaningful price/quality
directions: an affordable option and a higher-end option.

Concrete example: For a petite beige cardigan, show an affordable
version and a higher-quality/investment version so Lily can decide
whether durability/versatility justify the price.

Importance: Preference.

Status: Unclear.

Clarification or proposed change: Offer comparison tiers when credible
products exist. Do not equate price automatically with quality; use
available evidence and label uncertainty.

Missing/unclear 5 — Buy Now vs. persistent Save for Later

PRD section/requirement ID: FR-17; FR-37; Section 13.

Current plan: The data model distinguishes wishlisted and
owned/purchased states and preserves product context.

What I need: A very explicit product action: Buy Now or Save for Later,

Concrete example: Lily likes a cardigan but does not want to decide

Importance: Preference (high-value).

Status: Unclear as explicit UX; underlying state largely exists.

Clarification or proposed change: Expose the existing wishlist concept
as a straightforward Save for Later action in the shopping UI.

Missing/unclear 6 — Learn closet/style from Lily-created outfit photos

PRD section/requirement ID: FR-11; FR-19; FR-20; Section 9/9.1.

Current plan: The app learns known items and supports garment

What I need: Lily wants to upload a photo of an outfit she assembled

Concrete example: Lily creates a work outfit without AI, photographs

Importance: Nice to have / Preference.

Status: Unclear.

Clarification or proposed change: Explore assisted garment recognition
from an outfit photo, but require Lily to review/correct inferred items.
Do not infer exact size, brand, fabric, or fit when unknown.

Missing/unclear 7 — Primary visual consistency for generic closet items

PRD section/requirement ID: FR-08; FR-19; FR-37; Generate Look workflow.

Current plan: Generic imagery/placeholders are allowed and must be
labeled; actual-item try-on requires usable garment imagery for faithful

What I need: Even when Lily has entered only “blue dress pants” rather

close as reasonably possible and at minimum preserve known facts such as
color.

Concrete example: If the item is recorded as blue, the preview cannot
silently make it green.

Importance: Essential for trust in the visual experience.

Status: Unclear / needs explicit visual-fidelity requirement.

Clarification or proposed change: Define minimum fidelity for known
user-supplied attributes (color/category and other confirmed attributes)
while clearly labeling unknown/representative details. This remains a
preview, not proof of exact garment appearance or fit.

7. FEATURES I WOULD SIMPLIFY OR REMOVE

Remove/simplify “Creative/Risky” as a fixed Lily lane

For Lily, this label does not match her personality or desired workflow.
She wants intentional experimentation, not a “risky” identity.
Unexpected colors should live inside Stylish/Elevated.

Do not make detailed wear-date tracking prominent

Lily does not need the app to remember that she wore an outfit on a
particular Tuesday. She cares more about whether an outfit belongs in
Work, Gym, Going Out, etc.

Keep feedback lightweight

The PRD includes feedback such as too dressy/not my style/wrong
weather/don’t suggest again (FR-39). Lily did not reject those controls,
but her preferred first response to a bad piece is direct editing: tap
the garment, change the color/item, keep the rest. Do not make a heavy
rating flow stand between her and editing.

Keep setup incremental

Do not turn closet creation into a large prerequisite. Lily wants to add
clothes as she goes.

Keep the interface straightforward

Lily could not identify another feature she wanted removed, but
explicitly said “the more straightforward the better.” Advanced
implementation, privacy, reliability, backup, and legal features can
exist without making the everyday styling flow feel complicated.

9. THREE REALISTIC TASKS THAT WOULD PROVE APP USEFUL

Task 1 — Last-minute work interview

Situation: Lily has little time and owns blue dress pants. She needs an
interview-appropriate outfit and cannot shop first.

Input: - Occasion: work interview. - Starting piece: blue dress pants. -
Weather: current conditions. - Comfort: current fit preference. -
Shopping: no / current closet only.

Success: - App returns three useful visual options based only on
eligible owned clothes. - Options are shown on Lily in her preferred
visual mode. - At least one is safe and at least one is more
interesting/elevated. - The app does not simply repeat navy/blue
variations. - Lily can tap the cardigan/top and change the color while
preserving the rest of the look. - No nonexistent garment is presented
as owned.

Task 2 — Event with a color requirement and body-comfort constraint

Situation: Lily is going to an event where blue is the theme, it is
rainy, and she feels bloated.

Input: - Occasion: event. - Required/preferred color: blue. - Weather:
rainy. - Daily comfort: bloated / prefer less fitted. - Closet: use
known inventory.

Success: - All three looks honor the event/color/weather/comfort
constraints. - The outfits remain visually intentional rather than
merely making everything blue. - Lily can see the options on herself. -
She can edit one garment/color without resetting the outfit. - The app
clearly distinguishes permanent preferences from today-only context.

Task 3 — Decide whether a new petite piece is worth buying

Situation: Lily has time to shop and the elevated shopping option
suggests a beige cardigan (or another missing item).

Input: - Shopping allowed/requested. - Petite fit required. - Current
closet known enough to assess combinations.

Success: - Missing item is clearly labeled as not owned. - App shows how
the item could work with multiple known closet pieces. - Lily can see
several visual outfit ideas using it. - She can compare an affordable
option with a higher-end option when credible choices exist. -
Fit/size/length evidence and uncertainty are shown honestly. - She can
change the proposed item/color before searching. - She can choose Buy
Now or Save for Later. - Save for Later persists and does not mark the
item owned. - A purchase is not marked owned until Lily explicitly
confirms it.

OVERALL INTERVIEW CONCLUSION

The PRD’s fundamental direction—petite-first, closet-first, low setup,
visual styling, three choices, modular swaps, optional shopping, and
user-controlled data—matches Lily well.

The biggest product change suggested by the interview is not “more
features”; it is a sharper definition of what the styling experience is
for Lily:

She wants a visual, on-her-body stylist that understands what she owns
and what she needs TODAY, deliberately expands her color/styling choices
without becoming random or “risky,” lets her directly edit individual
pieces, and helps her build a more intentional wardrobe over time.

Shopping should support that goal rather than dominate it. When shopping
is useful, the app should prove why a piece earns a place in her
wardrobe by showing what she can do with it, respecting petite fit,
offering meaningful price/quality comparisons, and letting her postpone
the decision without losing the item.

All proposed PRD changes in this document remain feedback for
product/developer review, not automatic requirements.
